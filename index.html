Part 1

Quarks

What everything is made of

~400 BC: Democritus proposed that everything is made of tiny indivisible pieces, the atomos (“uncuttable”)

But atoms can be split: nucleus, protons, neutrons and, deep inside, quarks

Today: just 17 fundamental particles (quarks, leptons, force carriers, Higgs)

Engage the audience: ask how many layers they think there are. Each click zooms in: from an apple (10 cm) to an atom (a billion times smaller), to the nucleus (another 10,000 times smaller), to a proton, to a quark. Quarks and electrons show no size at all down to 10^-18 m.

Everyday matter

Everything around us is made of just three particles: protons, neutrons and electrons

They form the ~100 elements: (almost) all you need for chemistry and biology

Electrons are fundamental: no substructure has ever been seen

Protons and neutrons are not fundamental: they are made of smaller pieces

Keep it light; this is the familiar starting point. The element is fixed by the number of protons: 1 for hydrogen, 6 for carbon, 79 for gold.

Quarks with quirky names

Quarks are the building blocks of protons and neutrons, held together by the strong force

Proton = \(u + u + d\)
Neutron = \(u + d + d\)

“Three quarks for Muster Mark!”

Gell-Mann took the name from James Joyce’s Finnegans Wake

Four more quarks exist: charm \(c\), strange \(s\), beauty \(b\), top \(t\)

Mention the funny names. "Beauty" is the main character of this talk. The springs are gluons: they keep swapping the quarks' "colour" charge (the three colours). The force does not weaken when you pull quarks apart, so they never come out alone.

Nature repeats itself: flavour

Nature comes in three copies (“generations”) of quarks and leptons

The copies have the same charges and forces; only their masses differ

Flavour is the name for these copies

Generation 1 (\(u\), \(d\), \(e\), \(\nu_e\)) makes up all everyday matter

Generations 2 and 3 are heavier and unstable: they quickly decay into lighter particles

Transitions between different flavours are mediated by the $W$ boson

Rabi on the muon: "Who ordered that?" Nobody knows why there are three copies. Circle sizes follow the mass on a logarithmic scale. The matrix shows how strongly the W boson connects each pair of quarks (the CKM matrix): transitions inside one generation are strong, transitions between generations are weak.

The Standard Model of particle physics

Matter: 6 quarks and 6 leptons

Forces are carried by particles: photon \(\gamma\) (electromagnetic), gluon \(g\) (strong),
\(W\) and \(Z\) (weak)

The Higgs gives fundamental particles their mass

Masses span a huge range: \(m_\nu < 1\) eV, \(m_e = 0.5\) MeV, \(m_t = 173\) GeV (as heavy as a gold atom)

Antimatter: every particle has a twin with the same mass but opposite charge (\(e^- \leftrightarrow e^+\))

Stress the mass hierarchy: top quark ~340,000x electron. Unexplained. On the click for masses, the particles move onto a logarithmic mass axis: each tick is a factor 1000.

The SM success

\(W\), \(Z\), \(t\) and \(H\) were predicted, then found

Higgs discovered at CERN in 2012 (Nobel Prize 2013) using its decay to two photons

The bump is centred around the Higgs mass

\(N_\text{events} \propto \dfrac{1}{4\,(m_{\gamma\gamma}-m_H)^2+\Gamma_H^2}\)

The SM describes thousands of measurements with remarkable precision

Last click: the key message appears on its own. This is arguably the most precisely tested theory in science. The bump: for every event with two photons we compute their combined (invariant) mass from the photon energies E1, E2 and the opening angle theta. Most photon pairs come from unrelated processes and give a smoothly falling distribution. Higgs bosons always produce pairs with mass 125 GeV, so they pile up in one place on top of the background. The plot is an illustrative simulation with the shape of the 2012 ATLAS/CMS plots; the lower panel shows the data minus the background. 4th generation: the Z width measured at LEP gives 2.98 ± 0.01 light neutrino species; heavy 4th-generation quarks would also enhance Higgs production by gluon fusion about 9×, which the measured Higgs rates exclude. The SM has been tested over decades by hundreds of experiments.

The SM is not the ultimate theory of nature

Dark matter: ~5× more than ordinary matter, and not in the SM

Why did the Big Bang leave matter but no antimatter?

The flavour puzzle: why three generations, and why do they have these masses?

Gravity is not included

⇒ There must be New Physics

Transition: so how do we find what's missing? Pie: Planck satellite values (4.9% ordinary matter, 26.8% dark matter, 68.3% dark energy). Rotation curve: stars far from the centre of a galaxy orbit too fast for the visible mass alone; the gap is attributed to dark matter (schematic curve).

The beauty quark

~4.5× heavier than a proton: the heaviest quark that forms bound states

Quarks are never free: the strong force grows as you pull them apart ⇒ we study \(B\) mesons, a \(b\) quark bound to a light antiquark

A \(B\) meson lives ~1.5 × 10−12 seconds, yet flies a few mm at the LHC ⇒ a visible displaced vertex

b -> c is the most common B decay: the b quark turns into a c quark and a W boson, which becomes a lepton and a neutrino. The lifetime is long because the W connects the third and second generations only weakly (|Vcb| ≈ 0.04, the small number in the CKM matrix). At the LHC the B mesons move close to the speed of light, so time dilation stretches the 1.5 ps into a flight of millimetres to centimetres.

Part 2

Accelerators

Basic principle

Accelerate particles to speeds close to the speed of light and make them collide

Electric fields push charged particles; magnets steer and focus them

In the collision, their energy becomes matter (\(E = mc^2\)): new particles appear
higher energy ⇒ heavier particles

30,000+ accelerators worldwide, most in hospitals and industry (e.g. the IU Cyclotron Facility, 1975–2014)

Connect to everyday life and to IU. Electric fields speed the particles up; magnets keep the proton bunches on track and focused. At the collision point the kinetic energy is turned into the mass of new particles (E = mc^2): the higher the energy, the heavier the particles we can make. The IU Cyclotron Facility ran from 1975 to 2014 and also treated patients with proton therapy.

The purpose

Probe the structure: see what is inside particles, much as Rutherford did with the atom

Study the forces: electromagnetic, weak and strong

Produce new particles: collision energy becomes mass (\(E=mc^2\))

Test predictions: measure interactions and decays to test the SM and search for new physics

Rutherford (1909-1911): alpha particles shot at gold foil. Most go straight through, a few bounce back: the atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, dense nucleus. This is the template of every collider experiment: probe the structure by scattering.

The synchrotron

Particles go around a ring many times instead of once

Radio-frequency cavities give the particles an energy kick on every lap

The magnets get stronger as the energy grows ⇒ the particles stay on the same circle

Bending makes particles radiate (synchrotron radiation) ⇒ high energies need huge rings

Keep it conceptual. The LHC is a synchrotron. Click: dipole magnets bend the path of the bunches, quadrupoles focus them (like lenses). Every bend makes the particles radiate light; the lighter the particle and the higher the energy, the more they lose, which is why electron rings are so much larger than they need to be.

The LHC in numbers (I): the machine

A 27 km ring, ~100 m underground, on the border between France and Switzerland

The accelerator alone cost ~$5 billion (about 5 billion Swiss francs), built 1998–2008

Cooled to 1.9 K: colder than outer space (2.7 K)

Protons at 6.8 TeV move at 99.999999% of the speed of light: ~11,000 laps per second

Each proton bunch stores ~0.2 MJ: like a 1,000 kg car at ~70 km/h

CHECK all numbers against CERN 'LHC the guide' (Run 3 values). The vacuum in the pipes where the bunches travel is emptier than interplanetary space.

The LHC in numbers (II): the collisions

~2,400 proton bunches circulate in each direction, each with up to ~160 billion protons

Bunches cross every 25 ns, so up to 40 million times per second

~60 collisions per crossing ⇒ ~1.5 billion collisions per second, each creating ~100 particles

~\(10^6\) gigabytes of raw data per second: triggers keep only ~1 crossing in 10,000

~\(1.5 \times 10^9\) gigabytes stored on a computing grid of 170+ sites in 42 countries

CHECK numbers. 'Like finding a needle in a haystack, a billion times per second.' Each crossing produces hundreds to thousands of particles. The picture refreshes every second or so: every new picture is a different bunch crossing, with ~60 simultaneous collisions (pile-up); only one of them is the interesting one, drawn with coloured tracks.

The collaborations and the detectors

CERN: ~2,500 staff and 12,000+ visiting scientists from 70+ countries

ATLAS: 46 m long, 25 m tall, ~6,000 members (IU is part of ATLAS!)

CMS: 14,000 tonnes, heavier than the Eiffel Tower

LHCb: ~1,800 members, a detector built specifically to study \(b\) quarks

CHECK collaboration sizes. Emphasise global collaboration. The drawing is to scale (sizes: ATLAS 25 m tall, CMS 15 m, LHCb about 21 m long), with a 1.8 m person next to ATLAS.

Beauty quarks at the LHC

LHCb: ~1 million \(b\)-quark pairs every second, about \(10^{13}\) every year

ATLAS and CMS: general-purpose detectors, but their huge data samples make them strong for \(B\) decays with muons, e.g. \(B_s \to \mu^+\mu^-\)

Enough \(B\) mesons to see decays as rare as 1 in a billion

Production rate: about 10^5 bb pairs per second at LHCb's original luminosity (2 x 10^32), about 10 times more with the upgraded detector (2 x 10^33), i.e. ~10^6 per second and, over a year of data taking, of order 10^13 (about 10^12 pairs were produced in the acceptance in Runs 1 and 2). Bridge to the next part: what do we do with all these B mesons? LHCb looks only at the forward direction because the b quarks are produced mostly close to the line along which the bunches travel, in a narrow cone.

Not only the LHC: B factories

\(B\) factories collide electrons and positrons at just the right energy (10.6 GeV) to make a pair of \(B\) mesons

BaBar (SLAC, USA) and Belle (KEK, Japan)

Belle II (Japan), running since 2019: aims for ~50× more data than Belle

Fewer \(B\) mesons than at the LHC, but very clean events: only the two \(B\) mesons, so even decays with two invisible neutrinos can be studied

CHECK production rate. Bridge to the next part: what do we do with all these B mesons? At 10.6 GeV the collision makes the Upsilon(4S), which decays almost only into a B and an anti-B that are nearly at rest: no other particles, so we know the total energy and can infer invisible neutrinos.

Part 3

New Physics

The loops in the background are virtual particle pairs: they pop out of the vacuum and disappear again, all the time. This is how heavy new particles can show up without being produced, the theme of this part.

Two ways to find new particles

Direct searches: create new particles in collisions (\(E = mc^2\)) and look for a bump

⇒ needs ever bigger, more energetic colliders

Indirect searches: look for their small effects on known processes

⇒ needs very precise measurements and predictions

The two are complementary, and indirect searches often see hints first

Introduce 'precision' as the theme of the talk. Top: a direct search. If the collider has enough energy to make the new particle, it shows up as a bump on top of the SM expectation, like the Higgs did. Bottom: an indirect search. We measure a known process very precisely and compare with the SM prediction (the band is its uncertainty). If the measurements settle away from the band, something not in the SM contributes, even if it is far too heavy to be produced.

Seeing the invisible

1846: Neptune was found from tiny wobbles in Uranus’ orbit, before anyone saw it directly through a telescope

In quantum field theory, heavy new particles can appear briefly (“virtually”) in interactions of known particles

Even if they are too heavy to produce, they leave small fingerprints in what we measure

Energy-time uncertainty in plain words: you can 'borrow' energy for a short time. Neptune: Le Verrier (and independently Adams) computed where an unseen planet had to be to explain the wobbles of Uranus; Galle found it on 23 September 1846, within 1° of the prediction. The bulge of the orbit in the picture is exaggerated. Bottom: two known particles exchange a heavy particle for a very short time. The heavier it is, the shorter the time and the smaller its effect: it shows up as a tiny change in what we measure.

Flavour has done it before

1970, GIM: some kaon decays were missing ⇒ predicted the charm quark (found 1974)

1973, Kobayashi–Maskawa: CP violation (matter vs antimatter) ⇒ predicted a 3rd generation (Nobel 2008)

1987, ARGUS: \(B\)-meson mixing was large ⇒ the top quark must be very heavy (found 1995 at Fermilab)

Each time, flavour predicted new particles before they were seen

Strong selling point: flavour has an excellent track record. GIM (Glashow, Iliopoulos, Maiani): a fourth quark cancels the kaon decays that were not seen (K_L → μ⁺μ⁻); the J/ψ, a charm-anticharm pair, was found in November 1974 at SLAC and Brookhaven. Kobayashi and Maskawa: CP violation, seen in kaons in 1964, needs at least three generations. The b quark followed in 1977 (Fermilab), the top in 1995. ARGUS at DESY: B mesons turn into their antiparticles much faster than expected, so the top must be very heavy. It turned out to be 173 GeV.

Rare beauty decays

\(B \to K \mu^+\mu^-\) decays are ideal to look for New Physics

Theory perspective

In the SM, a \(b\) quark cannot turn into an \(s\) quark directly

Only possible through quantum loops ⇒ very rare

Fewer than one \(B\) meson in a million decays into \(K \mu^+\mu^-\)

Experimental perspective

A clean signature: two muons and a kaon, easy to measure and with small backgrounds

Analogy: hearing a whisper is easier in a quiet room. Rare decays are the quiet room. Build-up: first the direct jump (forbidden), then the loop with the W boson and the top quark, then the photon or Z that turns into the two muons. B → K μ⁺μ⁻ has a branching ratio of about 5 × 10^-7, B → K* μ⁺μ⁻ about 10^-6. The name: John Ellis lost a game of darts to Melissa Franklin and had to put the word "penguin" into his next paper.

Rare decays and new particles

A new heavy particle, e.g. a \(Z'\), can add its own contribution to the decay

The SM part is tiny ⇒ the new particle competes on equal terms ⇒ a large relative effect

It only appears “virtually”, so it can be heavier than the LHC can produce

Its effect shrinks like \(1/M^2\), but precise measurements reach ~100 TeV

We see a deviation, not the particle itself

Analogy: hearing a whisper is easier in a quiet room. Rare decays are the quiet room. The Z′ is a heavier cousin of the Z boson that could couple differently to the three generations. In b → s μ⁺μ⁻ it acts directly (no loop needed), while the SM needs a loop: that is why even a very heavy Z′ can compete. The ~100 TeV reach assumes the new particle couples with roughly the strength of the known forces; for weaker couplings the reach is lower.

Part 4

Calculations

The equations drifting in the background are from our paper arXiv:2206.03797 (Gubernari, Reboud, van Dyk, Virto): the effective operators, the form factors, the conformal variables and the dispersive bound. Nobody has to read them: the point is that the calculation behind one prediction fills a whole paper.

Measure mesons, learn about quarks

Study: the quark transition \(b \to s\,\mu^+\mu^-\)

⇒ this is where new physics would show up

We measure: the meson decay \(B \to K\,\mu^+\mu^-\)

⇒ quarks are always locked inside mesons

Inside a meson, the quarks sit in a swarm of gluons and quark–antiquark pairs that constantly pop in and out

How the whole \(B\) meson turns into a \(K\) meson is called the hadronic matrix element: the hardest part of the prediction

Analogy: we want to follow a conversation (the quarks) but we can only listen through a wall (the meson). To understand what was said, we must know how the wall muffles the sound. The quark-level part (W, top, new physics) is calculable with pen and paper to about 1%. The hadronic matrix elements are described by a few functions, the form factors, which depend on q^2, the mass squared of the muon pair. They are the main source of theory uncertainty.

Beyond perturbation theory

Our usual tool is perturbation theory: start from a simple answer and add small corrections, one by one

In electromagnetism (\(\alpha \approx 1/137\)) each correction is ~100× smaller: it works

Inside a meson the strong force is close to 1

Each correction is as large as the last ⇒ the series is useless

⇒ We need non-perturbative methods:
lattice simulations and sum rules

Analogy: predicting the flight of a paper plane by adding the effect of each gust of wind one at a time works in a light breeze, not in a storm. Strictly speaking the QED series is only asymptotic (it would eventually diverge, at an astronomically high order), but because each term is ~100 times smaller, the first few terms give an excellent approximation. In the strong case there is no such hierarchy, so we cannot truncate. Numbers: alpha_s(M_Z) = 0.118, about 0.3 at the b-quark mass, close to 1 at the scale of the proton. At very short distances the strong force becomes weak (asymptotic freedom, Nobel prize 2004 for Gross, Politzer, Wilczek): there perturbation theory works again. This is used by sum rules (next slides).

Lattice simulations on supercomputers

Replace space and time by a grid of points: quarks sit on the points, gluons on the links between them

The computer generates thousands of random configurations of the gluon field and averages over them

Then make the grid finer and the box bigger, and extrapolate to the real world

  • from first principles, systematically improvable
  • very expensive; hard when the \(K\) meson moves fast
Analogy: a weather forecast. The atmosphere is continuous, but the forecast is computed on a grid; a finer grid gives a better forecast and needs more computing power. Typical numbers: grid spacing below 0.1 fm, boxes of a few fm, about a billion numbers per configuration. Calculations run for months on the largest supercomputers. Invented by Ken Wilson in 1974. Lattice simulations of the strong interactions work best when the K meson is slow (large q^2); fast-moving light mesons need very fine grids.

Sum rules: one quantity, two descriptions

Idea: calculate the same quantity in two ways and set them equal

With quarks and gluons: at short distances, where perturbation theory works again

With hadrons: a sum over all particles that can appear in the final state: \(K\) meson + heavier ones

  • very powerful method: it can access matrix elements that lattice simulations cannot
  • large uncertainties due to the poorly known internal structure of the mesons
Sum rules were introduced by Shifman, Vainshtein and Zakharov (1979); light-cone sum rules followed in the late 1980s. The approximation in estimating the 'small payments' (heavier states, quark-hadron duality) gives an irreducible but quantifiable uncertainty of about 10-20%. Lattice and sum rules are complementary: sum rules work when the K meson is fast (small q^2), the lattice when it is slow (large q^2). My work: B-meson light-cone sum rules for B -> K, K* form factors [NG/Kokulu/van Dyk 2018, 1811.00983].

Part 5

Predictions

The grey wall is the kind of table that sits at the end of our paper arXiv:2206.03797: hundreds of predictions with their uncertainties, bin by bin and observable by observable. The coloured plots are what we compare with the data. Nobody has to read the numbers: the point is how much sits behind a single prediction.

Putting the pieces together

Sum rules work best when the \(K\) meson moves fast, the lattice when it moves slowly

We combine these calculations to extract matrix elements for any \(K\) meson speed

With these results we can predict observables that experiments can measure

q^2 is the mass squared of the muon pair: small q^2 means a fast K meson, large q^2 a slow one. We also add constraints from basic principles: probabilities cannot exceed 1 (unitarity). The result is a matrix element for every possible speed of the K meson, with 3-15% uncertainty. Unitarity bounds: the sum of the squared expansion coefficients must be smaller than 1. New parametrisation including subthreshold branch cuts [Gopal/NG 2025, 2412.04388]; latest results [NG 2026, 2605.26213], github.com/gubernari/suppl-unitb. EOS: open-source flavour physics software [2111.15428, 2610.03322]. The plot is schematic: the shape of a B -> K matrix element (form factor f_+) with the typical size of the uncertainties of each method.

The charm-quark trap

A second route: the \(b\) quark turns into a
charm–anticharm pair, which then emits a photon ⇒ \(\mu^+\mu^-\)

Same particles in the end ⇒ it can mimic new physics

Effect very difficult to calculate

We calculated it with sum rules, data and unitarity

⇒ lattice simulations are not available

B -> K J/psi has a branching ratio of about 1e-3 and J/psi -> mu mu about 6%: about 6e-5, compared with about 5e-7 for B -> K mu mu, so about 100 times more. Experiments cut out the regions around the J/psi and psi(2S) peaks, but the charm effect contributes everywhere. Technically these are 'non-local' matrix elements, the hardest part of the calculation [Khodjamirian et al. 2010; NG/van Dyk/Virto 2020, 2011.09813; NG/Reboud/van Dyk/Virto 2022, 2206.03797].

Theory meets experiment

We compare our predictions with measurements from LHCb, CMS, ATLAS and Belle

Measured rates are consistently below the SM predictions for \(B \to K\,\mu^+\mu^-\)

Tension of ~4 standard deviations

Similar tensions are seen in other \(b \to s\,\mu^+\mu^-\) transitions (\(B \to K^*\mu^+\mu^-\) and \(B_s \to \phi\,\mu^+\mu^-\))

5.7 sigma for B -> K mu mu + Bs -> mu mu, 2.7 sigma for B -> K* mu mu, 2.6 sigma for Bs -> phi mu mu [NG/Reboud/van Dyk/Virto 2022, 2206.03797]. The three data sets give consistent shifts of the same coupling (C9): a coherent pattern. A sigma value only means something if the theory uncertainties are under control: this is why the previous slides matter. The plot is redrawn from the EOS preliminary plot: SM band and measurements from LHCb 2014, BaBar 2012, Belle 2019 and CMS 2024. The numbers were read off the picture (pixel analysis), so they are accurate to a few percent but they are not the published values; the first bin and the charm-resonance region are left out. The y axis is the differential branching fraction: the probability that a B decays this way, per GeV^2 of q^2. The kaon speed (in the B rest frame) falls as q^2 grows: it is fast on the left and slow on the right, which is why lattice calculations only reach the right-hand end.

New physics or strong interactions?

New physics? a new heavy particle, e.g. a \(Z'\) boson, fits the data better than the SM

⇒ must agree with all other measurements

Underestimated strong interactions? e.g. a charm effect larger than we think

⇒ to be tested with better calculations

To decide: more data, better theory, more decays

Malaphoric Z' models [Allanach/NG 2025, 2409.06804] fit the data better than the SM. R_K = rate(B -> K mu mu)/rate(B -> K e e). In 2021 LHCb reported 3.1 sigma evidence for R_K != 1; in December 2022 a re-analysis with better control of misidentified hadrons found agreement with the SM. The tension in the muon rates alone survives, and depends directly on the theory predictions. Other decays: Lambda_b -> Lambda mu mu [Feldmann/NG 2024], B -> K nu nu at Belle II. The balance swings between the two explanations: with the data we have, we cannot yet tell them apart.
